\begin{abstract}
Quantum optimal control is often evaluated by whether a high-fidelity pulse implements a target operation, but an optimized waveform alone does not always show how the controlled evolution is organized. Here we study single-qubit gate design using a continuous physics-informed representation, in which the control fields, state trajectories, and gate duration are generated as parts of one controlled dynamical process and validated by independent propagation. We apply this formulation to two settings: ordinary rotation gates and a geometric phase gate. For rotation gates, the learned process organizes itself into the expected optimal-control structure. The gate time, pulse form, and division of work between control channels follow the geometry of the target rotation, showing that the representation is not merely fitting endpoint gates with arbitrary waveforms. The same process-level view also makes pulse constraints, out-of-plane synthesis difficulty, time cost, and probe information demand readable within a unified description. For the geometric gate, the continuous representation brings the path condition into the optimized object itself. This allows the main local bottleneck, namely rapid turning of the control direction, to be converted into a turning-aware feedback term, producing a phase-closed geometric $Z$ gate with negligible dynamical phase. The result shows that path-level geometric structure can be not only diagnosed, but also used to refine the control process. Together, these results show that continuous physics-informed representations can turn quantum control from pulse fitting into the organization, diagnosis, and refinement of a physically interpretable controlled evolution.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Quantum optimal control, Physics-informed neural network, Rotation gates, Geometric gates}

\section{Introduction}

In many complex physical problems, obtaining a high-quality numerical solution does not automatically mean that the solution has been understood \cite{Schmidt2009NaturalLaws,Brunton2016SINDy,Iten2020PhysicalConcepts,Cranmer2020SymbolicModels}. This is especially true in high-dimensional optimization problems, where numerical algorithms may efficiently produce excellent answers while the internal organization of those answers remains obscure. In many such problems, the variables that control the solution, the constraints that form the true bottlenecks, and the regularities hidden inside the optimized object remain unclear even after an excellent numerical answer has been found. In this sense, solving a problem is not the same as seeing through it.

Quantum optimal control provides a representative example. Its practical goal is clear: externally tunable fields should steer a quantum system through a desired evolution and ultimately implement a target quantum operation. For gate-design tasks, high-fidelity control solutions can indeed be found systematically, and existing methods are highly successful as numerical solvers, particularly once a time window and a pulse parametrization have been specified \cite{Brif2010QuantumControlReview,Glaser2015TrainingCat,Koch2022QuantumTechnologies,Khaneja2005GRAPE,Palao2003KrotovUnitary}. This practical success is consistent with the broader quantum-control-landscape view, which explains why good solutions can often be found under appropriate controllability and resource assumptions \cite{Rabitz2006Observables,Chakrabarti2007Landscapes}. Yet a high-performing pulse by itself does not necessarily reveal how the control process is organized. Why are some gates naturally faster than others? Why do different control channels assume different roles for different target gates? Why can some gates be learned from very little information, whereas others require substantially richer constraints? These are not merely post-optimization questions. They are part of what it means to physically understand quantum optimal control.

A useful way to phrase the issue is that quantum optimal control is not only the adjustment of a set of pulse parameters. What one ultimately wants to design is a controlled evolution process under dynamical and experimental constraints \cite{Peirce1988QuantumOCT,DAlessandro2007QuantumControl,Brif2010QuantumControlReview,Koch2022QuantumTechnologies}. The control fields determine how the system moves; the system follows trajectories induced by those fields \cite{Peirce1988QuantumOCT,Ramakrishna1995Controllability}; the target operation determines what transformation the process must realize; and the allowed duration and experimental restrictions determine which processes are physically meaningful or optimal. In the most common discrete-pulse formulation, however, this complete process is represented mainly through the pulse parameters \cite{Khaneja2005GRAPE,Palao2003KrotovUnitary,Glaser2015TrainingCat}. The pulse is the object directly varied by the optimizer, while the system trajectories are obtained afterward by a propagator. Consequently, even when excellent pulses are found, the relation between the controls, the induced motion, the gate objective, and the time scale can remain hidden inside the optimized waveform. This distinction is especially relevant when the scientific question is not only whether a control field exists, but which geometric or dynamical constraint explains its form \cite{Jurdjevic1972LieGroups,Ramakrishna1995Controllability,Boscain2006TimeMinimal,Garon2013TimeOptimalSU2,Zhang2023GeometricHolonomicReview}.

This work starts from this point and reformulates quantum optimal control using a continuous physics-informed representation. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) were originally developed to encode governing equations as differentiable constraints, and the broader physics-informed-learning literature emphasizes both their mesh-free continuous representation and their sensitivity to loss balance and training pathologies \cite{Raissi2019PINN,Karniadakis2021PIML,Cuomo2022PINNReview,Wang2021GradientPathologies,Krishnapriyan2021FailureModes}. Related continuous-depth and universal-differential-equation approaches likewise treat dynamical processes as differentiable objects rather than as fixed lists of samples \cite{Chen2018NeuralODE,Rackauckas2020UDE}. In quantum control, earlier PINN-based studies have shown that neural representations can produce high-fidelity state transfers or quantum gates, and related physics-informed work on dissipative quantum dynamics has emphasized the need to respect physical constraints rather than merely interpolate trajectories \cite{Guengordu2022SmoothPINNGates,Norambuena2024PINNQuantumControl,Lauten2025GateDesign,Ullah2024QuantumDissipative}. These works establish that PINNs can represent controlled quantum dynamics. The question addressed here is what this representation changes about the organization of the optimal-control problem itself.

Our formulation treats single-qubit gate design as the optimization of a continuous controlled process. The optimized object is no longer only a finite vector of pulse amplitudes. Instead, one trainable representation generates the control fields and the relevant trajectories as continuous functions of physical time, while the Bloch-equation residual, the terminal gate objective, the time cost, and pulse constraints are imposed within a unified differentiable objective. A central ingredient is that the total duration is also optimized as a time scale of the same process, rather than selected by an external scan. Control, evolution, and time are therefore not separated into independent subproblems; they are organized as different aspects of one controlled dynamical object. This is the step that allows the learned process itself to reveal time scales, channel allocation, and pulse structures, instead of only producing a waveform that implements the target.

Here we focus on a complementary question: once the control fields, the trajectories, and the duration are represented together, what physical organization appears in the learned process, and which parts of that organization can be used again as design information? The learnable duration brings the physical time scale into the same optimization as the fields and trajectories, while the geometric-gate construction brings path-local constraints into the same differentiable representation. In this way, the method is not only a different numerical route to a high-fidelity gate. It also provides a way to ask how an optimized quantum-control process organizes its time scale, control geometry, trajectory information, and local path errors.

We first address this question with ordinary rotation gates driven by the available $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$ controls.  Under only the Bloch dynamics, the terminal gate objective, the amplitude bounds, and the time cost, the learned processes organize into the expected bounded-control structure: the optimized duration follows the direct-control time scale, the pulse form approaches the saturated-control limit, and the two channels divide the rotation according to the target axis.  This agreement with standard SU(2) control theory is assessed only after the solutions are obtained, and shows that the continuous representation is not merely fitting endpoint gates with arbitrary waveforms.  The same rotation-gate family then provides a setting in which to vary the physical requirements within one controlled process.  Smooth and endpoint-compatible pulses reveal the time cost of experimental compatibility, while out-of-plane target axes reveal the cost of indirect synthesis through noncommuting controls and the corresponding increase in probe-information demand.  Thus the rotation-gate results serve one role: they show how a learned controlled process can organize, and then diagnose, the structure of endpoint quantum control.

We then use a geometric $Z$ gate to move from endpoint control to path-constrained control. Geometric gates are motivated by the fact that phases accumulated along an evolution can depend on the path, not only on the endpoint, and can provide built-in resilience against certain control errors \cite{Berry1984GeometricPhase,Aharonov1987CyclicPhase,Wilczek1984GaugeStructure,Zanardi1999HolonomicQC,Zhu2002NonadiabaticGeometric,Sjoqvist2012NonadiabaticHolonomic,Zhang2023GeometricHolonomicReview}. Experimental demonstrations across NMR, superconducting circuits, and solid-state spins further show why geometric gates are a genuine control problem rather than only a formal phase construction \cite{Jones2000GeometricNMR,Feng2013NHQCExperiment,Abdumalikov2013NonAbelian,Nagata2018HolonomicSpin}. In a geometric gate, the path condition is part of the operation itself: the reference trajectory must remain coordinated with the control direction so that the accumulated phase is geometric rather than dynamical. This makes the geometric gate a natural setting in which the controlled path, not only the final endpoint, has to be organized. In our learned solutions, the remaining geometric error is concentrated near rapid turns of the control direction, where the reference trajectory is hardest to keep in the required local relation. Because the controls and reference trajectories are generated inside one differentiable process, these difficult intervals can be given more weight during training, allowing the optimizer to readjust their coordination. The resulting controls realize a phase-closed geometric $Z$ gate with negligible dynamical phase, showing that path-level structure can be used not only for diagnosis but also for refining the control process.

The central claim of this work is therefore representational. Quantum control can be viewed not only as pulse optimization but as the design of a controlled dynamical process: fields, trajectories, constraints, and time must be understood together \cite{Peirce1988QuantumOCT,Brif2010QuantumControlReview,Koch2022QuantumTechnologies}. By representing this process continuously and enforcing the governing equation, the generated object is not only a pulse that can be validated after training, but also a set of physical quantities that can be organized, inspected, and modified. For rotation gates, this makes time scales, channel allocation, experimental constraints, and probe information visible within one process. For geometric gates, it allows local path errors to become differentiable design feedback. This complements endpoint-landscape views by asking what physical organization is present inside a successful control \cite{Rabitz2006Observables,Chakrabarti2007Landscapes}, and it illustrates how mechanistic information can be turned into differentiable structure that can be optimized and independently checked \cite{Raissi2019PINN,Chen2018NeuralODE,Rackauckas2020UDE}.

\section{Methods}

The method section explains how a quantum gate-control task is turned into an object that can be optimized.  The central point is that a control solution is not only a pulse waveform.  It is a complete controlled evolution: the applied fields determine how the system moves, the state trajectories describe the motion induced by those fields, the terminal map must reproduce the desired quantum gate, and the total duration fixes the physical time scale on which the process takes place.  Figure~\ref{fig:method_overview} is arranged around this viewpoint.  Panels (a,b) show how a GRAPE-type discrete-pulse representation separates these ingredients, while panels (c,d) show how our continuous physics-informed representation ties them together within one differentiable description.

We consider a driven open two-level system represented by a Bloch vector
\begin{equation}
    \rr(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))^{\mathsf T} .
\end{equation}
The two experimentally tunable channels enter through the control Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H_c(t)=\frac{\Omega(t)}{2}\sigma_x+\frac{\Delta(t)}{2}\sigma_z .
\end{equation}
Thus $\Omega(t)$ and $\Delta(t)$ are not merely numerical inputs to an optimizer; they are the physical knobs that determine the instantaneous rotation axis available to the qubit.  In the presence of relaxation and dephasing, the induced motion of the Bloch vector is governed by
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\rr}(t)=F[\rr(t),\Omega(t),\Delta(t)] ,
    \label{eq:bloch_main}
\end{equation}
with component form
\begin{align}
    \dot x&=-\Delta(t)y-\Gamma_2x,\\
    \dot y&= \Delta(t)x-\Omega(t)z-\Gamma_2y,\\
    \dot z&= \Omega(t)y-\Gamma_1 z+\gamma_\downarrow-\gamma_\uparrow .
\end{align}
These equations define how a proposed pair of control fields is converted into a physical trajectory.  Since the Hamiltonian contains only $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$, the directly controlled plane is the $x$--$z$ plane of the Bloch sphere.

The target operation is a one-qubit rotation
\begin{equation}
    U(\hat n,\varphi)=\exp\left[-\frac{i\varphi}{2}\hat n\cdot\bm\sigma\right] ,
\end{equation}
which acts on Bloch vectors through the corresponding rotation matrix $R(\hat n,\varphi)$.  In the main rotation-gate study we first choose a family whose axes lie in the directly controlled plane,
\begin{equation}
    \hat n=(\cos\alpha,0,\sin\alpha) .
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha$ parameterizes the direction of the desired rotation axis: $\alpha=0$ and $\alpha=\pi/2$ correspond to the $X$ and $Z$ directions, while intermediate values require the two control channels to cooperate.  We also include gates with nonzero $y$ component as out-of-plane cases, where the target axis cannot be directly aligned with either available Hamiltonian term.  Details of the Lindblad model, target rotations, and channel reconstruction are given in \appsecref{supp:physical_model}.

For a given gate task, the physical objects to be determined are therefore
\begin{equation}
    \{\Omega(t),\Delta(t)\},\qquad \{\rr^{(k)}(t)\}_{k=1}^4,\qquad T .
\end{equation}
The first set specifies how the system is driven, the second describes how a set of probe states moves under that drive, and the final scalar fixes the duration of the whole process on the physical time axis.  In this sense, the goal is to find a controlled evolution process that is dynamically valid, implements the desired gate, and respects the imposed physical constraints.

Here a probe state has a concrete operational meaning.  It is a selected initial Bloch vector used to test how one and the same control process acts on different inputs.  During a single gate optimization, the same controls $\Omega(t)$ and $\Delta(t)$ drive all selected probes.  In the main gate-learning objective we use four linearly independent probe initial states,
\begin{equation}
    \rin^{(1)}=\bm e_z,
    \qquad
    \rin^{(2)}=-\bm e_z,
    \qquad
    \rin^{(3)}=\bm e_x,
    \qquad
    \rin^{(4)}=\bm e_y .
\end{equation}
Each probe trajectory is therefore one input--output test of the same implemented operation [Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(b,d)].  The terminal states of the four probes are later used to reconstruct the affine map implemented by the pulse and to compare that map with the target channel through the gate loss; the complete construction is derived in \appsecref{supp:physical_model}.  The role of the probes is not to provide four unrelated training examples, but to check whether a single controlled evolution acts consistently on several independent initial states.

The standard GRAPE-type representation approaches this process by first choosing a total time $T$ and then projecting the controls onto a finite pulse basis \cite{Khaneja2005GRAPE,Glaser2015TrainingCat,Koch2022QuantumTechnologies}.  The interval $[0,T]$ is divided into $N$ bins, and the control fields are represented by their bin amplitudes [Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(a)],
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(t)=\Omega_j,
    \qquad
    \Delta(t)=\Delta_j,
    \qquad t\in[j\Delta t,(j+1)\Delta t) .
\end{equation}
The optimized variables are the pulse values themselves,
\begin{equation}
    \bm c_{\rm disc}=\{\Omega_j,\Delta_j\}_{j=0}^{N-1} .
\end{equation}
Once these amplitudes have been chosen, the state trajectories are no longer independent objects in the optimization.  They are obtained by propagating Eq.~\eqref{eq:bloch_main} under the piecewise-constant fields [Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(b)].  The dynamics are therefore enforced by the propagator, but the optimizer mainly acts on the pulse coordinates and evaluates the resulting terminal gate error.  For a fixed $T$, the objective can be written schematically as
\begin{equation}
    \Ldisc(\bm c_{\rm disc};T)=\beta_{\rm gate}\Lgate+\Lreg,
\end{equation}
where $\Lgate$ is constructed from the terminal states of four propagated probes and $\Lreg$ regularizes the pulse sequence [Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(b)].  Because the duration is not part of the optimized variables, different candidate values of $T$ must be treated by repeated optimizations.  The matrix-exponential propagation, L-BFGS-B optimization, restart strategy, and time-scan protocol used for this baseline are provided in \appsecref{supp:grape}.

Our representation starts from a different choice of optimized object [Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(c,d)].  Instead of treating the pulse amplitudes as the degrees of freedom, we introduce a continuous generator with parameters $\theta$.  These parameters are not the values of the control field at a finite set of time points.  Rather, they define a rule for generating an entire controlled process.  Once $\theta$ is specified, the control fields and all four probe trajectories can be evaluated at arbitrary physical times [Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(d)].  The collocation points used in training are therefore checkpoints at which the generated functions are tested against physics; they are not the objects being memorized.

For a physical time $t$, the neural network outputs two control heads and four trajectory heads,
\begin{equation}
    f_\theta(t)\mapsto
    \bigl(u_\Omega(t),u_\Delta(t),
    \bm u^{(1)}(t),\ldots,\bm u^{(4)}(t)\bigr),
    \label{eq:network_output_main}
\end{equation}
which is the joint representation shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(d).  The first two heads are mapped to bounded control fields,
\begin{align}
    \Omega(t)&=\Omega_{\max}\tanh u_\Omega(t),\\
    \Delta(t)&=\Delta_{\max}\tanh u_\Delta(t),
    \label{eq:control_param_main}
\end{align}
while the remaining heads define the four probe trajectories,
\begin{equation}
    \rr^{(k)}(t)=\rr_{\rm in}^{(k)}+g(t)\bm u^{(k)}(t),
    \qquad g(0)=0 .
    \label{eq:traj_param_main}
\end{equation}
In the implementation we use $g(t)=1-e^{-t}$, so the initial condition is built into the representation.  The important point is that the controls and the trajectories are generated by the same parameter set.  Updating $\theta$ changes the fields and the four probe paths together.  Thus the trajectory is no longer only an object computed after a pulse has been optimized; it is represented as part of the same controlled process as the pulse.

This joint representation is useful only if the generated fields and trajectories are physically consistent.  We impose that consistency by making the Bloch equation a closure condition on the generated process.  At physical collocation times $t_i$, the residual is
\begin{equation}
    \bm R^{(k)}(t_i)=
    \frac{\dd \rr^{(k)}}{\dd t}(t_i)
    -F[\rr^{(k)}(t_i),\Omega(t_i),\Delta(t_i)] .
    \label{eq:dyn_residual_main}
\end{equation}
Because the network is queried directly by the physical time $t$, automatic differentiation gives $\dd\rr^{(k)}/\dd t$ directly.  The dynamical loss is
\begin{equation}
    \Ldyn=\frac{1}{4N_s}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N_s}
    \left\|\bm R^{(k)}(t_i)\right\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:Ldyn_main}
\end{equation}
Compared with the propagator-based formulation, the role of the physical equation is different.  It is no longer hidden inside a forward propagation routine.  It appears explicitly in the objective and is therefore part of the differentiable feedback that updates the generator [Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(d)].

The gate objective is also expressed at the level of the whole process.  A one-qubit open-system evolution implements an affine map on the Bloch ball,
\begin{equation}
    \rout=M\rin+\bm c .
\end{equation}
A gate is not a condition on one special trajectory, but a transformation that must act correctly on all input states.  Because the four probe initial states above are chosen along coordinate directions, the implemented affine channel can be read directly from their terminal states.  If the terminal outputs of $+\bm e_z$, $-\bm e_z$, $+\bm e_x$, and $+\bm e_y$ are denoted by $\bm s_0$, $\bm s_1$, $\bm s_x$, and $\bm s_y$, respectively, then
\begin{align}
    \bm c&=\frac{\bm s_0+\bm s_1}{2},
    &M\bm e_z&=\frac{\bm s_0-\bm s_1}{2},\nonumber\\
    M\bm e_x&=\bm s_x-\bm c,
    &M\bm e_y&=\bm s_y-\bm c .
    \label{eq:main_channel_reconstruction}
\end{align}
Thus the two $z$-axis probes determine the translation and the third column of $M$, while the $x$- and $y$-axis probes determine the first two columns after subtracting the translation.  A derivation and explicit target matrices are given in \appsecref{supp:physical_model}.  The reconstructed channel is compared with the target channel $(\Mtar,\ctar)$ through
\begin{equation}
    \Lgate=\lambda_M\|M-\Mtar\|_F^2+
    \lambda_c\|\bm c-\ctar\|_2^2,
    \label{eq:Lgate_main}
\end{equation}
with $\ctar=0$ for the ideal target rotations used here.

Once the controlled process has been represented as continuous functions, the total duration can be introduced in a natural way.  This is a central advantage of the formulation.  In a discrete pulse representation, changing the duration changes the time grid on which the pulse amplitudes are defined, so the time is usually treated as an external hyperparameter and optimized only through a separate scan.  In the continuous representation, by contrast, the fields and trajectories are functions of physical time.  Changing the duration can therefore be understood as changing the physical time scale over which the same type of controlled process is queried.  The optimizer is not only asked what the fields should look like; it is also allowed to determine how much physical time the gate should occupy.

We implement this by introducing an unconstrained scalar $\tau$ and defining
\begin{equation}
    T=e^\tau .
    \label{eq:T_tau_main}
\end{equation}
The trainable variables are then $(\theta,\tau)$ rather than $\theta$ alone.  The network parameters $\theta$ define the continuous fields and trajectories, while $\tau$ defines the physical length of the interval on which these functions are evaluated.  To evaluate the process during training, we keep a fixed normalized set of labels $s_i\in[0,1]$ and map them to physical query points through the learnable duration [Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}(c)],
\begin{equation}
    t_i=s_iT=s_i e^\tau .
    \label{eq:ti_siT_main}
\end{equation}
The normalized labels $s_i$ are therefore only relative markers along the process.  The actual input to the network is the physical time $t_i$:
\begin{equation}
    f_\theta(t_i)\mapsto
    \{\Omega(t_i),\Delta(t_i),\rr^{(1)}(t_i),\ldots,\rr^{(4)}(t_i)\} .
    \label{eq:network_query_time_main}
\end{equation}
This simple mapping is what makes time optimization part of the same representation.  When $\tau$ changes, the endpoint $T$, the collocation times $t_i$, and the terminal readout all move together.  The time penalty is chosen as $\LT=T$: once the dynamical and gate constraints make a feasible process, this term biases the optimizer toward a shorter compatible duration.  Thus the duration is not a post-processing parameter added after a fixed-time pulse has been found.  It is a time-scale degree of freedom of the continuous controlled process itself.

The dependence on $\tau$ enters the same objective as the network parameters.  Backpropagation therefore provides both gradients,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \Ltotal}{\partial \theta},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \Ltotal}{\partial \tau},
    \label{eq:theta_tau_grad_main}
\end{equation}
within one optimization loop.  In this sense, learnable time changes the formulation of the control problem: the framework no longer asks only how to realize a target gate within a prescribed duration, but jointly searches for the continuous control fields, the compatible probe trajectories, and the physical time scale of the gate.  Implementation-level details of how $\tau$ enters the network queries, trajectory parametrization, dynamical residual, and terminal gate loss are given in \appsecref{supp:pinn}.

The total objective gathers the requirements above,
\begin{equation}
    \Ltotal(\theta,\tau)=
    \alpha_{\rm dyn}\Ldyn+
    \beta_{\rm gate}\Lgate+
    \lambda_T\LT+
    \Lreg,
    \label{eq:Ltotal_main}
\end{equation}
where $\LT=T$.  When pulse-shape regularization is used, we take
\begin{equation}
    \Lreg=
    \chi_{\rm amp}\mathcal L_{\rm amp}+
    \zeta_{\rm smooth}\mathcal L_{\rm smooth}+
    \eta_{\rm bnd}\mathcal L_{\rm boundary},
    \label{eq:Lreg_main}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L_{\rm amp}&=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i
    [\Omega(t_i)^2+\Delta(t_i)^2],\\
    \mathcal L_{\rm smooth}&=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i
    [\dot\Omega(t_i)^2+\dot\Delta(t_i)^2],\\
    \mathcal L_{\rm boundary}&=\Omega(0)^2+\Omega(T)^2+
    \Delta(0)^2+\Delta(T)^2 .
    \label{eq:reg_terms_main}
\end{align}
Backpropagation then differentiates the whole construction with respect to both $\theta$ and $\tau$.  In this way, the optimization updates the rule that generates the fields and trajectories, and also the physical duration over which that rule is evaluated.  Network architecture, collocation strategy, loss weights, and the automatic-differentiation chain are given in \appsecref{supp:pinn}.

Finally, the learned process is validated independently.  After training, we keep only the learned controls $\Omega(t),\Delta(t)$ and the learned duration $T$, and we propagate the Bloch equation with an external high-accuracy Runge--Kutta solver.  The independently propagated terminal states reconstruct the implemented channel.  In the unit-trace Choi, or equivalently trace-orthonormal Pauli process, convention we use
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm proc}=\operatorname{Tr}(\chi_{\rm tar}\chi_{\rm imp})
    \label{eq:Fproc_main}
\end{equation}
as the primary fidelity metric in the Results because it evaluates the implemented quantum channel itself, independently of any particular input state.  The corresponding single-qubit average gate fidelity is computed from the standard process-fidelity relation \cite{Nielsen2002AverageGateFidelity}, and random-state fidelity is also evaluated.  This separates the physics-informed representation used during training from the final dynamical validation of the learned control; details of the channel-fidelity convention and fourth-order Runge--Kutta (RK4) validation are provided in \appsecref{supp:validation}.

\section{Results}

The results follow a progression from organization to diagnosis and then to design feedback.  The rotation-gate family first shows how a continuous controlled process organizes its own time scale, pulse structure, and channel division under endpoint gate constraints.  The same family then shows how pulse-shape requirements, indirect synthesis, time overhead, and probe information appear as diagnostics of that process.  The geometric gate extends the idea to a path-defined operation: local path errors become physical objects inside the differentiable representation and can therefore be converted into local design pressure.


\subsection{Rotation gates: from direct-control geometry to indirect synthesis}

We first use ordinary rotation gates to ask how much structure a learned controlled process can organize under endpoint constraints alone.  The available Hamiltonian contains only the $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$ controls, so rotations whose axes lie in the $x$--$z$ plane form the directly generated family,
\begin{equation}
    \hat n(\alpha)=(\cos\alpha,0,\sin\alpha),
    \qquad
    \alpha\in[0,\pi/2].
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:rotation_axes} shows this family between $X(\pi)$ and $Z(\pi)$.  All results in this subsection are evaluated by independent RK4 propagation and channel reconstruction after training; the network-generated trajectories are used only during the physics-informed optimization.  To provide a process-level view of the resulting controls, Movie S1 of the Supplemental Material~\cite{supplemental} shows the time-resolved Bloch trajectories and synchronized control fields for a directly generated $X(\pi)$ gate and an indirectly synthesized $Y(\pi)$ gate.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_rotation_axes.pdf}
  \caption{Directly generated rotation axes in the controlled $x$--$z$ plane.  The family $\hat n(\alpha)=(\cos\alpha,0,\sin\alpha)$ interpolates between $X(\pi)$ and $Z(\pi)$ using the available $\Omega$ and $\Delta$ channels.}
  \label{fig:rotation_axes}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Direct-control organization in the minimal prescription}

The first question is whether the continuous representation recovers the intrinsic organization of the directly controlled plane when no pulse shape is prescribed.  We therefore use a minimal prescription: $\Omega_{\max}=\Delta_{\max}=8.0$, initial duration $T_0=0.7$, time-cost coefficient $\lambda_T=1$, and no smoothness, amplitude, boundary, or envelope regularization.  Numerical weights and collocation settings are collected in \appsecref{supp:pinn}.

Figure~\ref{fig:rotation_minimal} summarizes the learned solutions in the minimal prescription.  Panel (a) shows that all seven in-plane gates reach average gate fidelities above $99.3\%$.  The $X(\pi)$ solution uses an almost saturated $\Omega$ channel with $\Delta\simeq0$, the $Z(\pi)$ solution exchanges the two roles, and the near-$45^\circ$ rotation uses both bounded channels simultaneously.  Panel (b) shows the corresponding learned durations: $T^*$ forms a U-shaped curve with the shortest time near $\alpha=45^\circ$, where the two available channels share the rotation most efficiently.  Thus the pulse shape, the channel division, and the learned time scale all show the same direct-control geometry before any analytic ansatz is imposed.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_rotation_minimal.pdf}
  \caption{Minimal pulse-shape limit for directly generated gates.  (a) Representative controls for $X(\pi)$, $R(45^\circ,\pi)$, and $Z(\pi)$ recover saturated square-pulse structure without prescribing a square-pulse ansatz.  (b) The optimized durations for all seven in-plane gates follow the direct-control prediction in Eq.~\eqref{eq:T_direct_results}; the shaded band marks $\pm4\%$.}
  \label{fig:rotation_minimal}
\end{figure}

This structure has a simple analytical reference from Pontryagin's maximum principle.  For a bounded control problem, the minimum-time solution uses the available control amplitude as strongly as allowed.  In the present drift-free direct-control setting, this means that the fastest pulse is a saturated square pulse whose control vector is aligned with the desired rotation axis \cite{Boscain2006TimeMinimal,Garon2013TimeOptimalSU2,Boozer2012SU2Synthesis,Dionis2023PiecewiseTimeOptimal}.  For a direct rotation by angle $\varphi$ around $\hat n(\alpha)$, the vector $(\Omega,0,\Delta)$ must be parallel to $(\cos\alpha,0,\sin\alpha)$.  With independent bounds $|\Omega|,|\Delta|\leq\Omega_{\max}$, at least one channel saturates, giving
\begin{equation}
    T_{\rm direct}(\alpha)
    =
    \frac{\varphi\,\max(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)}{\Omega_{\max}} .
    \label{eq:T_direct_results}
\end{equation}
This is the reference curve plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:rotation_minimal}(b), and the optimized durations follow it to within a few percent.  In our optimization, however, no square-pulse ansatz, channel-allocation rule, or minimum-time formula is supplied.  The optimizer sees only the Bloch dynamics, the gate target, the amplitude bound, and the time cost.  The agreement with the PMP-based reference therefore shows that the continuous representation organizes the known bounded-control structure on its own, rather than inserting that structure as prior knowledge.

The same time law is obtained by the GRAPE-type baseline when the duration is scanned externally.  This provides an independent check of Eq.~\eqref{eq:T_direct_results}.  The distinction is where the time scale lives: in the continuous representation, $T$ is optimized jointly with the fields and trajectories, whereas in the discrete-pulse baseline each candidate duration requires a separate optimization (\appsecref{supp:grape}).

\subsubsection{Pulse constraints preserve the control geometry}

The minimal solutions reveal the intrinsic time law, but their square-like form is not the pulse shape usually preferred by hardware.  We next ask whether the same control geometry survives when the process is made smoother or endpoint-compatible.  We compare weakly regularized continuous controls, a sine-envelope prescription, and the piecewise-constant GRAPE-type baseline.  The regularization terms are those defined in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:Lreg_main} and \eqref{eq:reg_terms_main}; the prescription-level details are given in \appsecref{supp:pinn}.

Figure~\ref{fig:pulse_comparison}(a) shows that even weak amplitude, smoothness, and boundary penalties produce smooth endpoint-compatible controls in the continuous representation.  Figure~\ref{fig:pulse_comparison}(b) shows the corresponding GRAPE-type baseline used here: because the native variables are bin amplitudes, the optimized controls remain piecewise constant even under much larger amplitude and smoothness weights.  This comparison is a contrast between the representations used in this paper, not a claim that conventional control cannot employ smooth parametrizations.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_pulse_comparison.pdf}
  \caption{Pulse-shape contrast between the continuous representation and a GRAPE-type baseline.  (a) With weak regularization, $\chi_{\rm amp}=10^{-4}$, $\zeta_{\rm smooth}=10^{-4}$, and $\eta_{\rm bnd}=10^{-3}$, the continuous representation produces smooth endpoint-compatible controls for $X(\pi)$, $Z(\pi)$, and $Y(\pi)$.  (b) GRAPE controls remain piecewise constant even under much larger amplitude and smoothness regularization, $\chi_{\rm amp}=0.05$ and $\zeta_{\rm smooth}=0.5$.}
  \label{fig:pulse_comparison}
\end{figure}

Although the pulse shape changes, the channel allocation remains geometric.  If the target axis lies in the $x$--$z$ plane and the control vector follows that axis, then
\begin{equation}
    \frac{|\Omega|}{|\Delta|}
    \simeq
    \frac{\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}
    =
    \cot\alpha .
    \label{eq:division_law}
\end{equation}
The regularized solutions follow this trend across the directly generated family [Fig.~\ref{fig:allocation_law}].  Thus the weakly regularized pulses do not create a new allocation rule; they preserve the same direct-control geometry in a smoother implementation.  The angle $\alpha$ controls both the minimal duration in Fig.~\ref{fig:rotation_minimal} and the channel division in Fig.~\ref{fig:allocation_law}.

The cost of making the pulses smoother and endpoint-compatible is quantified in Fig.~\ref{fig:pulse_shape_cost}.  For the in-plane $X$ and $Z$ gates, the main cost is an increase in optimized duration, with only a slight decrease in average fidelity.  The $Y(\pi)$ gate is more sensitive: its duration increases more strongly and its fidelity decreases more visibly.  This difference points to the indirect-synthesis problem considered next.  Unlike $X$ and $Z$, the $Y$ axis is perpendicular to the directly controlled $x$--$z$ plane and must be synthesized indirectly through the noncommuting action of $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_allocation_law.pdf}
  \caption{Geometric channel-allocation law.  Under regularized controls, the peak-amplitude ratio follows $|\Omega|_{\max}/|\Delta|_{\max}\simeq\cot\alpha$, showing that smoothing the pulse does not create the allocation law artificially.}
  \label{fig:allocation_law}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Indirect synthesis increases time and information demand}

We next consider target rotations outside the directly controlled plane.  The Hamiltonian contains only $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$, so a target component along $y$ must be generated indirectly through the commutator structure $[\sigma_x,\sigma_z]=2i\sigma_y$, reflecting the Lie-algebraic structure of coherent control \cite{DAlessandro2007QuantumControl}.  We therefore ask how this indirect synthesis appears inside the learned process.

Figure~\ref{fig:ny_time} shows the time-scale signature of this indirect synthesis.  In addition to the seven in-plane gates with $|n_y|=0$, we include four rotations with nonzero $n_y$.  The optimized minimal-prescription duration increases systematically with the out-of-plane component, and the $Y(\pi)$ gate, with $|n_y|=1$, has the largest duration.  Thus the target-axis geometry has two complementary roles: the in-plane angle $\alpha$ controls direct speed and channel division, while $|n_y|$ measures the burden of indirect synthesis.

The same direct-versus-indirect distinction appears in the amount of trajectory information needed during training.  The standard formulation uses four probe trajectories to reconstruct the affine channel.  We also train a reduced version with a single carefully chosen terminal probe while keeping the same architecture, dynamical residual, optimizer, and independent four-probe RK4 validation; the protocol is given in \appsecref{supp:pinn}.  All seven in-plane gates are learned successfully from one probe, with average fidelities comparable to the four-probe results after full validation.  By contrast, the out-of-plane gates fail under the same reduced constraint.  This is not a uniqueness statement, since one input-output constraint cannot determine a general one-qubit gate.  Rather, for directly generated gates, one informative trajectory plus the Bloch residual gives enough signal for the optimizer to find a control that passes the full channel test.  For gates with nonzero $n_y$, the essential object is the coordinated noncommuting action of $\Omega(t)$ and $\Delta(t)$, and one trajectory does not specify that coordination strongly enough.

Finally, a dissipative ablation clarifies the boundary of what coherent control can compensate in this single-qubit setting.  We compare optimizing with the dissipative Bloch equation against optimizing with the corresponding closed-system dynamics, while evaluating both under the full dissipative dynamics.  Across dissipation strengths, anisotropies, and pulse prescriptions, explicit Lindblad optimization does not produce a systematic fidelity advantage; differences remain at the $10^{-4}$--$10^{-6}$ level.  The physical reason is that relaxation and dephasing shrink the Bloch ball irreversibly, and unitary controls cannot recover purity that has already been lost.  This ablation is therefore a diagnostic boundary case rather than a separate control mechanism.

Taken together, the rotation-gate results form a consistent progression.  In the minimal prescription, the learned process organizes into the direct-control time law, pulse form, and channel allocation.  Under pulse-shape constraints, the same geometry survives while acquiring an implementation cost.  For target axes outside the controlled plane, the cost becomes indirect synthesis and increased information demand.  The continuous representation therefore makes endpoint-control structure visible first as an organization principle and then as a diagnostic for how that structure changes under physical requirements.

The rotation-gate results above concern endpoint-constrained operations: the final affine map defines the target gate, while many intermediate paths can in principle realize the same endpoint.  We next consider a case in which the path itself is part of the operation.  This provides a natural setting to ask whether the same continuous representation can diagnose and modify constraints that live along the trajectory, not only at the final time.

\subsection{Geometric gates: from path diagnosis to feedback design}

We use a geometric $Z$ gate as the representative path-constrained task.  Its terminal action is the usual Bloch rotation $(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,-y,z)$, but this endpoint condition is not by itself a geometric implementation.  The reference states must also form closed paths and satisfy the local geometric condition that removes the dynamical phase.  Thus the correctness of the task has two parts: the probe trajectories verify the terminal $Z$ operation, while the reference trajectories verify the geometric character of the path.  Movie S2 of the Supplemental Material~\cite{supplemental} gives a time-resolved view of this path dynamics, including the Bloch-sphere trajectories, synchronized control fields, and instantaneous control direction.

For a pure geometric phase gate, the dynamical phase should vanish and the operation should be carried by the geometry of a closed path.  In the Bloch representation this is expressed by the parallel-transport condition
\begin{equation}
    E(t)=\langle\phi(t)|H(t)|\phi(t)\rangle=0,
    \qquad 0\leq t\leq T ,
    \label{eq:geometricity_results}
\end{equation}
or equivalently by orthogonality between the instantaneous control axis and the reference-state direction.  The cycle condition requires the reference state to return to the same ray at $t=T$.  Figure~\ref{fig:geometric_paths} summarizes the two roles of the trajectories: the probe states implement the target $Z$ rotation, whereas the reference states form the closed paths that carry the geometric constraint.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_geometric_paths.pdf}
  \caption{Path structure of the geometric $Z$ gate.  Reference states follow closed loops that carry the geometric phase, while probe states implement the target $Z$ rotation, $|{+}x\rangle\rightarrow|{-}x\rangle$ and $|{+}y\rangle\rightarrow|{-}y\rangle$.  Green arrows show representative control-field directions $\bm h(t)=(\Omega,0,\Delta)$.}
  \label{fig:geometric_paths}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Path-constrained evolution and local coordination errors}

The geometric task requires several constraints to hold at once.  All trajectories must satisfy the Bloch dynamics, the probe trajectories must reproduce the terminal gate, and the reference trajectories must satisfy geometricity, cycle closure, purity, and orthogonality, while the controls remain regularized.  In the main geometric-gate runs these requirements are combined schematically as
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L_{\rm geom,total}={}&
    \lambda_{\rm dyn}\Ldyn+
    \lambda_{\rm gate}\Lgate \nonumber\\
    &+\lambda_{\rm geo}\mathcal L_{\rm geo}+
    \lambda_{\rm cyc}\mathcal L_{\rm cyc} \nonumber\\
    &+\lambda_{\rm pur}\mathcal L_{\rm pur}+
    \lambda_{\rm orth}\mathcal L_{\rm orth} \nonumber\\
    &+\lambda_{\rm amp}\mathcal L_{\rm amp}+
    \lambda_{\rm smooth}\mathcal L_{\rm smooth}+
    \lambda_T\LT .
    \label{eq:geom_total_results}
\end{align}
The complete definitions and weights are given in \appsecref{supp:geometry}.  These terms impose competing requirements.  The geometric condition may require the control direction to reorient quickly, whereas smoothness penalizes rapid changes; cycle closure restricts the available paths, while gate accuracy keeps the terminal map fixed.

This competition can also create a training shortcut.  If the geometricity term is made dominant before the trajectories are dynamically meaningful, the network can reduce the algebraic quantity $E(t)=\bm h(t)\cdot\bm n(t)$ without producing a controlled path that remains valid under independent propagation.  We therefore use the staged schedule described in \appsecref{supp:geometry}: the dynamics and terminal gate action are organized first, and the geometricity weight is increased only after the controlled process has become physically consistent.

With this schedule and learnable time, the two-control geometric $Z$ gate reaches $F_{\rm proc}>0.999999$ with $T^*\simeq1.5$ under independent RK4 validation.  The remaining geometricity error is not spread uniformly along the path.  As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:turning_bottleneck}, it appears mainly near intervals where the control-field direction turns rapidly.  We quantify the direction of the control vector $(\Omega(t),0,\Delta(t))$ by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \psi(t)&=\operatorname{atan2}[\Delta(t),\Omega(t)],\\
    \kappa(t)&=|\dot\psi(t)|
    =\frac{|\Omega\dot\Delta-\Delta\dot\Omega|}
    {\Omega^2+\Delta^2+\epsilon_\kappa}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:kappa_results}
\end{equation}
Here $\epsilon_\kappa$ is a small numerical floor used only to avoid singular weights when the control amplitude is close to zero.  Because $\Omega(t)$ and $\Delta(t)$ are differentiable outputs of the network, $\dot\Omega$, $\dot\Delta$, and $\kappa(t)$ are computed directly by automatic differentiation.  The pointwise geometricity error aligns with peaks in $\kappa(t)$, while its correlation with the control amplitude is weak [Fig.~\ref{fig:turning_bottleneck}].

This alignment has a direct physical interpretation.  When the control direction turns quickly, the reference trajectory must adjust quickly as well in order to keep $\bm h(t)\perp\bm n(t)$.  These are the intervals where the required local geometric relation is hardest to maintain.  The error is therefore not simply an instantaneous amplitude error in the pulse; it is a local mismatch between how the control direction turns and how the reference path follows it.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_turning_bottleneck.pdf}
  \caption{Local geometricity error in the geometric $Z$ gate.  For the baseline learnable-time solution, peaks in the turning rate $\kappa(t)$ align with localized peaks in the geometricity error $|E(t)|$.}
  \label{fig:turning_bottleneck}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Weighted geometricity loss and discrete-control comparison}

This local diagnosis can be incorporated into the optimization.  Since the controls and reference trajectories are generated inside one differentiable process, the objective can give more weight to the times where their coordination is hardest to maintain.  We replace the uniform geometricity penalty by
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L_{\rm geo}^{\rm turn}
    =
    \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i
    \left[1+\beta\,\kappa_{\rm norm}(t_i)\right]
    \left[E_+(t_i)^2+E_-(t_i)^2\right],
    \label{eq:turn_weighted_results}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_{\rm norm}$ is normalized and treated as a weight.  The role of this term is not to suppress turning itself.  Rather, it asks the optimizer to maintain the geometric relation more carefully during the rapidly turning intervals identified in Fig.~\ref{fig:turning_bottleneck}.  The representative weights used for the continuous PINN and GRAPE-type comparisons are listed in \appsecref{supp:geometry}.

In the continuous representation, this modification gives the optimizer a direct signal to readjust how the control direction and reference path move together.  The correction can involve not only a local change in amplitude, but also a redistribution of how the control direction turns, how the trajectory follows it, and how the geometric relation is maintained along the path.

The $\beta$ scan shows this effect [Fig.~\ref{fig:turn_weighted_loss}(a)].  The mean pointwise geometricity error decreases and the correlation between $|E(t)|$ and $\kappa(t)$ weakens up to $\beta\leq30$, while the process fidelity remains above $0.999999$.  At $\beta=50$, the geometricity error rises again, indicating that excessive weighting disrupts the balance among geometricity, smoothness, and gate accuracy.  The representative weighted solution more accurately maintains $\hat{\bm h}\perp\hat{\bm n}$ during the rapidly turning intervals [Fig.~\ref{fig:turn_weighted_loss}(b,d)].

We also apply the same weighting strategy to the piecewise-constant GRAPE-type baseline at the matched duration $T=1.5$.  In that representation, the controls are constant inside each bin and discontinuous at bin boundaries.  A finite-difference turning rate therefore contains artificial boundary jumps and no structure inside a bin.  Increasing the turn weight from $\beta=0$ to $\beta=3$ keeps the terminal fidelity high, but the RK4-evaluated mean geometricity error increases from $4.28\times10^{-3}$ to $4.90\times10^{-3}$ and the peak error increases from $3.31\times10^{-2}$ to $6.50\times10^{-2}$ [Fig.~\ref{fig:turn_weighted_loss}(c,e)].  This comparison is limited to the native piecewise-constant baseline and the finite-difference turn measure used here; it is not a statement that conventional optimal-control methods cannot include path-dependent penalties.  In the continuous representation used here, the local geometric relation is part of the object being optimized, so the same diagnosis is more directly converted into a design signal.

\section{Conclusion and Discussion}

The results support a representation-centered view of quantum control.  A successful optimized pulse is not by itself the full physical object; it is one projection of a controlled process that also contains trajectories, constraints, and a time scale.  For rotation gates, representing these objects together lets the learned process organize into the expected bounded-control structure and then reveals how this structure changes under pulse constraints, indirect synthesis, and reduced probe information.  For the geometric gate, the same representation makes a path-local geometric error visible during optimization and allows the difficult intervals to be weighted during training.  In this sense, the two examples show the same principle at two levels: endpoint-control structure can be organized and diagnosed, while path-level structure can also be used to refine the design.

This perspective places the work as a complement to standard quantum-control methods rather than as a replacement for them.  Mature approaches such as GRAPE, Krotov-type algorithms, GOAT-like analytic-control methods, and CRAB-type smooth basis parametrizations remain powerful tools for producing high-fidelity controls in fixed-time or parametrized problems \cite{Khaneja2005GRAPE,Palao2003KrotovUnitary,Goerz2019KrotovPackage,Brif2010QuantumControlReview,Glaser2015TrainingCat,Machnes2018GOAT,Caneva2011CRAB,Koch2022QuantumTechnologies}.  The structures discussed here can also be obtained in other ways, for example by analytic reasoning, time scans, post-hoc trajectory analysis, or by choosing smooth pulse parametrizations from the beginning.  The distinction is not what is mathematically possible in conventional control, but which quantities are native to the formulation.  In a pulse-vector representation, trajectories, local path errors, and time-scale diagnostics are usually consequences of propagation.  In the continuous process representation, selected physical quantities are part of the optimized object itself, which makes them easier to inspect, constrain, and feed back into the design.

The same distinction is important for physics-informed learning.  The message is not simply that PINNs can synthesize quantum gates, nor that a small training residual should be trusted as physical correctness.  Soft physics constraints are known to be sensitive to loss imbalance, shortcut solutions, and optimization pathologies \cite{Karniadakis2021PIML,Cuomo2022PINNReview,Wang2021GradientPathologies,Krishnapriyan2021FailureModes}.  For this reason, the learned controls are validated by independent propagation and channel reconstruction rather than by the training loss alone.  After this validation step, however, the differentiability of the representation becomes scientifically useful: it allows local residuals, trajectory errors, and path-level constraints to be analyzed not only as diagnostics, but also as quantities that can reshape the objective.

Several limitations remain.  First, the present study is restricted to a single qubit, where the Bloch-vector picture and probe-based channel reconstruction are especially transparent.  Extending the approach to multiqubit gates will require more scalable descriptions of the implemented channel, for example operator-level losses, randomized probes, process-tensor constructions, or tensor-network-inspired representations.  Second, the hardware constraints considered here are still idealized.  Real devices include bandwidth limits, transfer functions, calibration nonlinearities, leakage, and noise spectra that should eventually be incorporated into the controlled process itself.  Third, the learnable-time formulation finds an optimized duration within a chosen parametrization, loss balance, and training protocol; it should not be read as a proof of global time optimality.  Finally, because the dynamics are imposed through soft residuals, balancing dynamical accuracy, gate fidelity, time cost, and regularization remains a practical issue rather than an automatic consequence of the formulation.

A natural next direction is therefore a hybrid program.  Conventional quantum-control solvers can provide efficient high-fidelity candidate pulses, while continuous physics-informed representations can be used to analyze their time scales, trajectory structure, and path-local difficulty.  For rotation gates, this suggests studying anisotropic amplitude bounds, drift terms, and experimentally measured transfer functions.  For geometric gates, it suggests extending the path-error analysis to multilevel leakage, non-Abelian holonomies, and robustness objectives that separate genuine geometric protection from ordinary pulse smoothing.

In conclusion, we have formulated single-qubit gate design as the optimization of a continuous controlled process, in which the control fields, state trajectories, and gate duration are generated and constrained together.  For ordinary rotation gates, the learned processes recover the expected bounded-control organization: the optimized time, pulse form, and channel allocation follow the geometry of the target rotation, and the same framework shows how this structure changes under smoothness constraints, endpoint compatibility, indirect out-of-plane synthesis, and reduced probe information.  For the geometric $Z$ gate, local geometric errors can be diagnosed along the reference trajectory and fed back into training by emphasizing the difficult intervals where the control direction turns rapidly.  These results support a representation-centered view of quantum optimal control: a useful control solution should be not only high fidelity, but also physically interpretable, diagnosable, and refinable.

\section{Physical model and target-gate definitions}
\label{supp:physical_model}

Starting from the Lindblad master equation \cite{Gorini1976CompletelyPositive,Lindblad1976Generators}
\begin{equation}
    \dot\rho=-i[H_c(t),\rho]
    +\gamma_\downarrow \mathcal D[\sigma_-]\rho
    +\gamma_\uparrow \mathcal D[\sigma_+]\rho
    +\frac{\gamma_\phi}{2} \mathcal D[\sigma_z]\rho,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D[L]\rho=L\rho L^\dagger-\frac{1}{2}L^\dagger L\rho-\frac{1}{2}\rho L^\dagger L,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    H_c(t)=\frac{\Omega(t)}{2}\sigma_x+\frac{\Delta(t)}{2}\sigma_z,
\end{equation}
we expand the density matrix as
\begin{equation}
    \rho(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[I+x(t)\sigma_x+y(t)\sigma_y+z(t)\sigma_z\right].
\end{equation}
The resulting Bloch equations are
\begin{align}
    \dot x&=-\Delta y-\Gamma_2x,\\
    \dot y&=\Delta x-\Omega z-\Gamma_2y,\\
    \dot z&=\Omega y-\Gamma_1 z+\gamma_\downarrow-\gamma_\uparrow,
\end{align}
with
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_1=\gamma_\downarrow+\gamma_\uparrow,
    \qquad
    \Gamma_2=\frac{\gamma_\downarrow+\gamma_\uparrow}{2}+\gamma_\phi .
\end{equation}

A target rotation gate is
\begin{equation}
    U(\hat n,\varphi)=\exp\left[-\frac{i\varphi}{2}\hat n\cdot\bm\sigma\right],
\end{equation}
and acts on Bloch vectors through the rotation matrix
\begin{equation}
    R(\hat n,\varphi)=I_3\cos\varphi+(1-
    \cos\varphi)\hat n\hat n^{\mathsf T}+\sin\varphi[\hat n]_\times,
    \label{eq:target_rot_supp}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    [\hat n]_\times=
    \begin{pmatrix}
    0&-n_z&n_y\\
    n_z&0&-n_x\\
    -n_y&n_x&0
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For the in-plane family used in the main text,
\begin{equation}
    \hat n=(\cos\alpha,0,\sin\alpha),
\end{equation}
which includes the $X$ and $Z$ rotations at the two endpoints and intermediate rotations within the directly controlled plane.  Rotations with nonzero $n_y$ are used as out-of-plane cases.

The learned open-system process is represented as an affine channel on the Bloch ball,
\begin{equation}
    \rout=M\rin+\bm c.
\end{equation}
For the four probes used in the main text, the input vectors are the coordinate directions
\begin{equation}
    +\bm e_z,
    \qquad -\bm e_z,
    \qquad +\bm e_x,
    \qquad +\bm e_y.
\end{equation}
Let their terminal outputs be
\begin{equation}
    \bm s_0,
    \qquad \bm s_1,
    \qquad \bm s_x,
    \qquad \bm s_y.
\end{equation}
Applying the affine map to each probe gives
\begin{align}
    \bm s_0 &= M\bm e_z+\bm c,\label{eq:s0_affine}\\
    \bm s_1 &=-M\bm e_z+\bm c,\label{eq:s1_affine}\\
    \bm s_x &= M\bm e_x+\bm c,\label{eq:sx_affine}\\
    \bm s_y &= M\bm e_y+\bm c.\label{eq:sy_affine}
\end{align}
Adding and subtracting the first two equations gives the displacement and the third column of $M$,
\begin{equation}
    \bm c=\frac{\bm s_0+\bm s_1}{2},
    \qquad
    M\bm e_z=\frac{\bm s_0-\bm s_1}{2}.
\end{equation}
The remaining two columns are obtained by subtracting the displacement,
\begin{equation}
    M\bm e_x=\bm s_x-\bm c,
    \qquad
    M\bm e_y=\bm s_y-\bm c.
\end{equation}
Because the inputs are coordinate-basis vectors, this reconstruction uses only the terminal outputs.  If a different, nonorthogonal probe set were used, one would instead solve the corresponding linear system for the affine map.

For a target unitary rotation $U(\hat n,\varphi)$, the target Bloch map is the $3\times3$ rotation matrix
\begin{equation}
    \Mtar=R(\hat n,\varphi),
    \qquad
    \ctar=0.
\end{equation}
The condition $\ctar=0$ reflects the fact that an ideal unitary gate is a rotation of the Bloch sphere about the origin, with no translation.  Dissipative Lindblad evolution can in general produce an affine displacement and contraction, but the target operation in this work remains the ideal rotation; dissipation is treated as part of the implemented process to be modeled and diagnosed rather than as a desired target translation.  For example,
\begin{align}
    X(\pi):\quad &\Mtar=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1),\nonumber\\
    Z(\pi):\quad &\Mtar=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1).
\end{align}
More generally, each target matrix is obtained from Eq.~\eqref{eq:target_rot_supp}.

\section{Traditional GRAPE-type discrete-pulse formulation}
\label{supp:grape}

This section gives the complete discrete-pulse baseline used as a comparison with the continuous PINN representation.  In the rotation-gate tasks we use a GRAPE-type method with L-BFGS-B \cite{Zhu1997LBFGSB}.  The same discrete framework can also be extended by adding geometric constraints for the geometric-gate comparisons.

For a fixed total time $T$, the interval $[0,T]$ is divided into $N$ bins,
\begin{equation}
    t_j=j\Delta t,
    \qquad
    \Delta t=\frac{T}{N},
    \qquad
    j=0,1,\ldots,N.
\end{equation}
The controls are piecewise constant,
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(t)=\Omega_j,
    \qquad
    \Delta(t)=\Delta_j,
    \qquad
    t\in[t_j,t_{j+1}).
\end{equation}
To impose amplitude bounds we use the unconstrained variables $u_j,v_j$ and the maps
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_j=\Omega_{\max}\tanh u_j,
    \qquad
    \Delta_j=\Delta_{\max}\tanh v_j .
\end{equation}
The number of trainable variables is therefore $2N$.

In the discrete method, trajectories are not directly optimized.  For each time bin, the controls are constant, so the Bloch equation is an affine linear ODE.  Introducing the augmented vector $\tilde{\rr}=(x,y,z,1)^{\mathsf T}$, the equation can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\tilde{\rr}=K_j\tilde{\rr},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    K_j=
    \begin{pmatrix}
    -\Gamma_2&-\Delta_j&0&0\\
    \Delta_j&-\Gamma_2&-\Omega_j&0\\
    0&\Omega_j&-\Gamma_1&\gamma_\downarrow-\gamma_\uparrow\\
    0&0&0&0
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The fourth row keeps the augmented component equal to one and absorbs the nonhomogeneous term into a homogeneous system.  The one-step propagation is
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\rr}_{j+1}=\Phi_j\tilde{\rr}_j,
    \qquad
    \Phi_j=\exp(K_j\Delta t).
\end{equation}
Composing the $N$ propagators gives the terminal state for each probe.  Applying this to the four probes yields the terminal outputs used to reconstruct $(M,\bm c)$.

The gate loss is the same channel-level loss defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Lgate_main}.  There is no dynamical-residual term in the discrete baseline, because the trajectories are computed by matrix-exponential propagation and therefore satisfy the Bloch equation for the chosen piecewise-constant pulse.  The total objective is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L={}&\beta_{\rm gate}\Lgate+
    \chi_{\rm amp}\mathcal L_{\rm amp} \nonumber\\
    &+\zeta_{\rm smooth}\mathcal L_{\rm smooth}+
    \lambda_{\rm boundary}\mathcal L_{\rm boundary}.
\end{align}
In the rotation-gate baseline we use $\beta_{\rm gate}=10.0$, $\chi_{\rm amp}=0.05$, $\zeta_{\rm smooth}=0.5$, and $\lambda_{\rm boundary}=1.0$.

Gradients are computed through the differentiable propagation chain.  The $N$ matrix-exponential propagation steps form a computation graph, and automatic differentiation gives gradients of the loss with respect to all pulse parameters $u_j,v_j$.
The optimizer is L-BFGS-B \cite{Zhu1997LBFGSB}, a quasi-Newton method widely used in GRAPE-type optimization.  The function tolerance is $10^{-12}$ and the gradient tolerance is $10^{-8}$.  Each line search is allowed up to 50 steps, and the maximum number of iterations is 500.

Because pulse optimization can contain multiple local optima, each gate is optimized with five independent restarts.  The first restart uses a small near-zero initialization with pulse parameters of order $0.1$, which favors low-amplitude smooth solutions.  The remaining four restarts use random initializations sampled uniformly from $[-1.5,1.5]$.  The best solution, measured by the gate loss, is kept.

In the GRAPE-type baseline, $T$ is fixed externally.  Although one could formally include $T$ as a variable, in practice changing $T$ changes $\Delta t=T/N$, all propagation matrices $\Phi_j=\exp(K_j\Delta t)$, and the accumulation of gradients through the entire propagation chain.  We therefore determine the optimal duration by an external time scan.

For a target rotation $U(\hat n,\varphi)$, we define a direct-control reference time $T_{\rm direct}$ as the bounded-channel reference scale in Eq.~\eqref{eq:T_direct_results}.  This provides a physically motivated reference scale for the scan.  We scan
\begin{align}
    T&=\eta T_{\rm direct},\\
    \eta&\in\{0.85,0.90,0.95,1.00, \nonumber\\
    &\hspace{1.2em}1.05,1.10,1.20,1.35\}.
\end{align}
For each candidate $T$, five restarts are performed, and the best result over all $T$ is selected.

After optimization, the best discrete pulse is validated with the same independent RK4 procedure used for PINN controls.  The four probe terminal states reconstruct $(M,\bm c)$, and the average gate fidelity and random-state fidelity are evaluated identically.  Table~\ref{tab:implementation_comparison} summarizes the rotation-gate implementation-level comparison between the discrete baseline and the continuous representation.

For the geometric-gate comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:turn_weighted_loss}, we use the same native piecewise-constant representation rather than smoothing the GRAPE pulse for plotting or validation.  The duration is fixed to the matched value $T=1.5$, the interval is divided into $N=80$ bins, and the probe and reference trajectories are propagated under the step controls.  The turn rate used in the weighted loss is computed from finite differences of the bin controls with the same small denominator floor used in the continuous calculation.  We compare the unweighted case $\beta=0$ with the stable weighted case $\beta=3$ and then re-evaluate the geometricity error with the same independent fourth-order Runge--Kutta validation used for the PINN controls.  This baseline tests a finite piecewise-constant implementation of a turn-weighted path-local objective; it is not intended as a statement about all conventional optimal-control parametrizations.

\section{Neural-network representation and training procedure}
\label{supp:pinn}

This section supplements the main-text description of the continuous PINN framework.  It gives the network architecture, the physical parameterization, collocation strategy, constraint levels, optimization hyperparameters, and automatic-differentiation chain.

The network is a six-layer fully connected multilayer perceptron with width 256 and $\tanh$ activations.  The input is the physical time $t\in[0,T]$, obtained from the normalized reference grid by $t_i=s_iT$.  The hidden layers are
\begin{equation}
    h_0=t,
    \qquad
    h_\ell=\tanh(W_\ell h_{\ell-1}+b_\ell),
    \qquad \ell=1,\ldots,6,
\end{equation}
followed by a linear output layer
\begin{equation}
    u(t)=W_{\rm out}h_6+b_{\rm out}\in\mathbb R^{14}.
\end{equation}
The output is divided into two control heads and four three-dimensional trajectory heads,
\begin{align}
    u(t)=(&u_\Omega(t),u_\Delta(t),
    u_x^{(1)}(t),u_y^{(1)}(t),u_z^{(1)}(t),\ldots, \nonumber\\
    &u_x^{(4)}(t),u_y^{(4)}(t),u_z^{(4)}(t)).
\end{align}
The use of $\tanh$ activations keeps the representation smooth and differentiable.  Xavier/Glorot initialization is used \cite{Glorot2010Understanding}.  No dropout or batch normalization is used, since the collocation set is fixed and the physical residual itself provides regularization.

The control fields are parameterized as
\begin{equation}
    \Omega(t)=\Omega_{\max}\tanh u_\Omega(t),
    \qquad
    \Delta(t)=\Delta_{\max}\tanh u_\Delta(t).
\end{equation}
A hard-envelope variant can also be used when the controls must vanish exactly at the endpoints,
\begin{align}
    \Omega(t)&=\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)
    \Omega_{\max}\tanh u_\Omega(t),\\
    \Delta(t)&=\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)
    \Delta_{\max}\tanh u_\Delta(t).
\end{align}
The trajectory for probe $k$ is written as
\begin{equation}
    \rr^{(k)}(t)=\rr_{\rm in}^{(k)}+g(t)\bm u^{(k)}(t),
    \qquad
    g(t)=1-e^{-t}.
\end{equation}
The condition $g(0)=0$ enforces the initial state exactly.  Since $g$ is evaluated at physical time, changing $T$ changes the envelope values at the collocation points.

The normalized collocation points $s_i\in[0,1]$ are uniformly distributed and fixed throughout training.  The physical query points are $t_i=s_iT$.  For the rotation-gate tasks, $N_s=121$.  Keeping the same normalized grid makes loss values comparable across training steps, while the learnable $T$ changes the corresponding physical sampling locations.

We distinguish three pulse-shape prescriptions.  The minimal setting keeps only the core dynamical, gate, and time terms with bounded controls, so the optimized pulse can approach the square-pulse bounded-control reference limit.  The regularized setting uses the regularization terms defined in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:Lreg_main} and \eqref{eq:reg_terms_main}.  The sine-envelope setting replaces the soft boundary penalty by a hard multiplicative $\sin(\pi t/T)$ envelope.  Typical rotation-gate weights are $\alpha_{\rm dyn}=1.0$, $\beta_{\rm gate}=10.0$, $\chi_{\rm amp}=10^{-4}$, $\zeta_{\rm smooth}=10^{-4}$, and $\eta_{\rm bnd}=10^{-3}$ when the hard envelope is not used.  The weights are chosen so that no single loss term dominates the gradient at the beginning of training.

For rotation gates, Adam \cite{Kingma2015Adam} is used with learning rate $10^{-3}$ for the network parameters.  In learnable-time experiments, $\tau$ uses a separate learning rate $5\times10^{-3}$ because it is a single scalar with a gradient scale different from the high-dimensional network parameters.  The training length is 3000 steps.  The reported rotation-gate runs use initial time $T_0=0.7$, amplitude bounds $\Omega_{\max}=\Delta_{\max}=8.0$, and time-cost coefficient $\lambda_T=1.0$ unless otherwise stated.

Geometric-gate training uses the same network representation but a separate path-constraint schedule; those details are collected in \appsecref{supp:geometry}.

This section spells out the computation graph used for the learnable-time implementation.  The normalized collocation points $s_i$ are fixed reference markers, but they are not the inputs to the neural network.  At each training step, the current scalar $\tau$ defines
\begin{equation}
    T=e^\tau,
    \qquad
    t_i=s_iT .
\end{equation}
The physical time tensor $t_i$ is then passed directly to the network,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    (\theta,\tau)&\longrightarrow T=e^\tau
    \longrightarrow t_i=s_iT\\
    &\longrightarrow f_\theta(t_i)
    \longrightarrow
    \{\Omega(t_i),\Delta(t_i),\rr^{(k)}(t_i)\}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:supp_time_chain}
\end{equation}
This is different from a normalized-time formulation in which one would call $f_\theta(s)$ and then convert derivatives using $(1/T)\dd/\dd s$.  In the present implementation, automatic differentiation is applied directly with respect to the physical input $t$, so that
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\rr}^{(k)}(t_i)
    =\frac{\partial \rr^{(k)}(t)}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=t_i}
\end{equation}
without an additional manual $1/T$ factor.  The Bloch residual is therefore evaluated as
\begin{equation}
    \bm R^{(k)}(t_i)=
    \dot{\rr}^{(k)}(t_i)
    -F[\rr^{(k)}(t_i),\Omega(t_i),\Delta(t_i)] .
\end{equation}

The gradient with respect to $\tau$ is still nontrivial, because $\tau$ changes the physical time scale on which the whole network-generated process is queried.  Schematically,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \Ltotal}{\partial \tau}
    =\sum_i
    \frac{\partial \Ltotal}{\partial t_i}
    \frac{\partial t_i}{\partial T}
    \frac{\partial T}{\partial \tau}
    +
    \left(\frac{\partial \Ltotal}{\partial T}\right)_{\rm explicit}
    \frac{\partial T}{\partial \tau},
    \label{eq:supp_tau_grad_schematic}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial t_i}{\partial T}=s_i,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial T}{\partial\tau}=T .
\end{equation}
The first term summarizes all paths through the physical query points: the network outputs $\Omega(t_i)$, $\Delta(t_i)$, and $\rr^{(k)}(t_i)$; the envelope $g(t_i)=1-e^{-t_i}$ used to impose the initial condition; and the dynamical residual evaluated at those times.  The explicit $T$ dependence includes the terminal readout at $t=T$, the time cost $\LT=T$, and any hard envelope or boundary term that depends on the endpoint.  Thus $\tau$ is coupled to the same forward graph as the network parameters, rather than being optimized in a separate outer loop.

During training, the optimizer receives both $\partial\Ltotal/\partial\theta$ and $\partial\Ltotal/\partial\tau$.  Because $\theta$ is a high-dimensional vector whereas $\tau$ is a single scalar, the two gradients generally have different scales; this is why the learnable-time experiments use a separate learning rate for $\tau$.  A typical training trajectory has three stages.  Early training primarily reduces the dynamical residual; mid training reduces the gate loss as the fields adjust to implement the target operation; late training balances the physical residual, gate objective, regularization, and time cost, with $T$ stabilizing near the optimized value.

\section{Independent validation and evaluation metrics}
\label{supp:validation}

After training, we validate the learned controls using an external fourth-order Runge--Kutta integrator and do not use the network-internal trajectories.  For each probe initial state, the update is
\begin{equation}
    \rr_{n+1}=\rr_n+\frac{\delta t}{6}
    (\bm k_1+2\bm k_2+2\bm k_3+\bm k_4),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
    \bm k_1&=F(\rr_n,\Omega_n,\Delta_n),\\
    \bm k_2&=F\left(\rr_n+\frac{\delta t}{2}\bm k_1,
    \Omega_{n+1/2},\Delta_{n+1/2}\right),\\
    \bm k_3&=F\left(\rr_n+\frac{\delta t}{2}\bm k_2,
    \Omega_{n+1/2},\Delta_{n+1/2}\right),\\
    \bm k_4&=F(\rr_n+\delta t\bm k_3,
    \Omega_{n+1},\Delta_{n+1}).
\end{align}
We use $N_{\rm RK4}=4000$, which is much larger than the number of training collocation points.  For PINN controls, the continuous generator is queried on the RK4 grid whenever the trained network is available; when diagnostic figures are generated from archived high-resolution control samples, interpolation is used only to reconstruct the exported control curve for validation and not as part of the training residual.  For GRAPE controls, the native piecewise-constant bin value is used at each RK4 stage.

The pointwise trajectory error is
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{\rm traj}=\frac{1}{N_pN_s}
    \sum_{k=1}^{N_p}\sum_{i=1}^{N_s}
    \left\|\rr_{\rm PINN}^{(k)}(t_i)-\rr_{\rm val}^{(k)}(t_i)\right\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
The channel fidelity is evaluated from the independently propagated terminal states.  These states reconstruct the affine Bloch map $(M,\bm c)$.  Equivalently, the trace-preserving single-qubit channel can be written in Pauli-transfer form as
\begin{equation}
    R_{\rm imp}=
    \begin{pmatrix}
    1&\bm 0^{\mathsf T}\\
    \bm c&M
    \end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    R_{\rm tar}=
    \begin{pmatrix}
    1&\bm 0^{\mathsf T}\\
    \bm 0&\Mtar
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For an ideal unitary target, the process fidelity reported throughout the Results is evaluated as the entanglement fidelity
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm proc}=\frac{1+\operatorname{Tr}(\Mtar^{\mathsf T}M)}{4}.
    \label{eq:fproc_ptm_supp}
\end{equation}
This expression is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm proc}=\operatorname{Tr}(\chi_{\rm tar}\chi_{\rm imp}).
\end{equation}
Here $\chi_{\rm tar}$ and $\chi_{\rm imp}$ are written as unit-trace Choi matrices, or equivalently in a trace-orthonormal Pauli process convention.  The affine displacement $\bm c$ is included in the gate loss and in the random-state validation; for a trace-preserving channel, it does not enter the entanglement fidelity with respect to a unitary target in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fproc_ptm_supp}.
For a single qubit, the corresponding average gate fidelity follows from the standard process-fidelity relation \cite{Nielsen2002AverageGateFidelity},
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm avg}=\frac{2F_{\rm proc}+1}{3}.
\end{equation}
We also sample 200 random Bloch vectors uniformly from the Bloch sphere, propagate them under the learned controls, and average their state fidelities with the ideal target outputs.  This directly tests the gate on generic input states.


\section{Additional implementation details for geometric-gate tasks}
\label{supp:geometry}

For the geometric-gate extension, the same continuous representation and learnable-time structure are retained, but additional path-level constraints are added.  The gate is evaluated with the four probe trajectories exactly as in the rotation-gate tasks, while two separate reference trajectories enforce the geometric path conditions.  In the Bloch-vector implementation, the network output therefore contains two control heads, four three-component probe-trajectory heads, and two three-component reference-trajectory heads, for a total output dimension of 20.

If the reference paths are described by Bloch vectors $\rr_+(t)$ and $\rr_-(t)$, with corresponding pure states $\phi_+(t)$ and $\phi_-(t)$, the instantaneous Hamiltonian vector is
\begin{equation}
    \bm h(t)=(\Omega(t),0,\Delta(t))^{\mathsf T},
\end{equation}
and the instantaneous geometricity error is
\begin{equation}
    E_\pm(t)=\langle \phi_\pm(t)|H(t)|\phi_\pm(t)\rangle
    =\frac{1}{2}\bm h(t)\cdot\rr_\pm(t).
\end{equation}
The geometricity loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L_{\rm geo}=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i
    \left[E_+(t_i)^2+E_-(t_i)^2\right].
\end{equation}
The cycle-closure loss penalizes failure of the reference paths to close:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L_{\rm cyc}=\sum_{a=\pm}
    \|\rr_a(T)-\rr_a(0)\|_2^2 .
\end{equation}
We also monitor and, when needed, penalize purity and orthogonality of the reference pair,
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L_{\rm pur}&=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{i,a=\pm}
    \left(\|\rr_a(t_i)\|_2^2-1\right)^2,\\
    \mathcal L_{\rm orth}&=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i
    \left[\rr_+(t_i)\cdot\rr_-(t_i)+1\right]^2 .
\end{align}
Thus the complete geometric-gate objective used in the main text has the form
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L_{\rm geom,total}={}&
    \lambda_{\rm dyn}\Ldyn+
    \lambda_{\rm gate}\Lgate \nonumber\\
    &+\lambda_{\rm geo}\mathcal L_{\rm geo}+
    \lambda_{\rm cyc}\mathcal L_{\rm cyc}\nonumber\\
    &+\lambda_{\rm pur}\mathcal L_{\rm pur}+
    \lambda_{\rm orth}\mathcal L_{\rm orth}\nonumber\\
    &+\lambda_{\rm amp}\mathcal L_{\rm amp}+
    \lambda_{\rm smooth}\mathcal L_{\rm smooth}+
    \lambda_T\LT .
\end{align}
For the representative two-control geometric $Z$ gate, we use $\lambda_{\rm dyn}=80$, $\lambda_{\rm gate}=50$, $\lambda_{\rm cyc}=80$, $\lambda_{\rm amp}=10^{-4}$, and $\lambda_{\rm smooth}=10^{-4}$.  The geometricity weight is introduced by a warm-up schedule: during the first 30\% of training we use a lower value $\lambda_{\rm geo,init}=10$, then increase it to the target value $\lambda_{\rm geo}=300$.  The purity and orthogonality terms are used as auxiliary path-quality penalties when the corresponding constraints are not already enforced structurally; their weights are chosen on the same scale as the cycle constraint in the reported runs.  We use a six-layer width-256 network, Adam \cite{Kingma2015Adam} with cosine annealing from $3\times10^{-4}$ to $10^{-6}$, 20000--30000 training steps, $N_s=201$, amplitude bounds $\Omega_{\max}=\Delta_{\max}=12.0$, and gradient clipping with maximum norm 5.0.

The staged schedule is important.  If the geometricity term is made too strong before the trajectories have become dynamically meaningful, the network can reduce the algebraic quantity $E_\pm(t)$ without producing a physically valid controlled path.  In the schedule used here, dynamical consistency, terminal gate accuracy, and cycle closure dominate early training; the geometricity weight is then increased after the controlled process has organized itself.

The turning-rate diagnostic measures how quickly the control direction changes in the $\Omega$--$\Delta$ plane.  Defining
\begin{equation}
    \psi(t)=\operatorname{atan2}[\Delta(t),\Omega(t)],
\end{equation}
we use
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(t)=|\dot\psi(t)|
    =\frac{|\Omega(t)\dot\Delta(t)-\Delta(t)\dot\Omega(t)|}
    {\Omega(t)^2+\Delta(t)^2+\epsilon_\kappa} .
\end{equation}
Large $\kappa(t)$ indicates that the control axis is rapidly changing direction.  The turn-weighted geometricity loss emphasizes precisely these intervals:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L_{\rm geo}^{\rm turn}=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i
    [1+\beta\kappa_{\rm norm}(t_i)]
    \left[E_+(t_i)^2+E_-(t_i)^2\right],
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_\kappa$ is set to a small numerical floor in the reported calculations and $\kappa_{\rm norm}$ denotes a normalized turning-rate weight.  The normalized weight is treated as a weight rather than as a quantity whose gradient is back-propagated through the geometricity loss; in practice it is normalized by the maximum or a robust high percentile of $\kappa$ after excluding endpoint points where the envelope can make the control amplitude nearly vanish.  In the continuous PINN scan, we compare $\beta=0$, moderate values up to $\beta=30$, and an over-weighted case $\beta=50$.  The main text reports that $\beta\leq30$ improves the geometricity error while maintaining $F_{\rm proc}>0.999999$, whereas $\beta=50$ increases the error and gate time.  For the GRAPE-type comparison, $\beta=3$ is used as the best stable attempt: larger values such as $\beta=30$ further amplify finite-difference spikes at bin boundaries and degrade the discrete-control geometricity signal.

The geometric-gate experiments compare two-control versus three-control implementations, hard versus soft constraints, fixed-time versus learnable-time optimization, and versions with or without turn weighting.  These variants are not separate algorithms; they impose different path-level constraints within the same continuous physics-informed representation.
